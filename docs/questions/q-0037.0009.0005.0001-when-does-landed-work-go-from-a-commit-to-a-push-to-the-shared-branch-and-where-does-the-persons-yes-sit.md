## Discovery, 2026-10-14

### Families

1. **Trunk-based, committing straight to trunk.** R and J are one act; S often. No person gates J:
   the author's discipline before R, CI detecting after S, fast repair or revert; unfinished work
   kept out by flags. Protects against late integration; accepts a bad change reaching the line.
   Ladder: direct commit is the small-team end, legitimate, not immature (medium-high). Fails:
   broken trunk blocks everyone, flags pile up, review vanishes or moves after the commit (high).
   DORA; trunkbaseddevelopment.com.
2. **Short-lived branch plus pull request (GitHub Flow).** R and S free on a personal branch, S
   being backup, sharing and CI trigger; J gated by PR, required checks, approvals, branch
   protection. Protects the shared line from unreviewed or untested change, leaves a record of why.
   Solo: self-merged PR, ceremony without protection. Fails: PRs grow large and long, review
   latency, rubber stamps, green-on-branch red-on-main. Two machines: the pushed personal branch is
   the usual carrier; the hazard is rewriting history on one and force-pushing over the other,
   `--force-with-lease` the mitigation (high).
3. **Ship / Show / Ask** (Wilsenach, 2021). The author picks per change: commit straight to main,
   PR merged at once with review after, or PR awaiting approval. R and S free. Fails: depends on
   honest classification, drifts to Ship under pressure; disagrees with family 2 that the author
   may judge their own change (medium).
4. **Pre-commit review of patches** (Gerrit, Phabricator, stacked diffs). The unit is the commit; S
   goes to a review queue, not a shared line; J only after approval and verification. Protects every
   commit on the line as reviewable and bisectable. Large-organisation tooling; a solo person is off
   its ladder. Fails: tooling overhead, restacking pain, reviewer availability (medium).
5. **Integration by robot** (the not-rocket-science rule, merge queues). No person performs J; a bot
   tests the candidate merge and advances main only if green. Protects main from ever being red,
   semantic conflicts included. For one person: latency for a race that rarely happens. Fails:
   slow or flaky tests stall it, emergency bypasses break it (medium-high).
6. **Release-branch models** (Git Flow). Adds a fourth movement: joining the integration line and
   joining the release line are separate. Protects what customers run from unstabilised work. Its
   own author (2020) puts continuously delivered software outside its range. Fails: long branches,
   forgotten back-merges, ceremony; anti-correlated with delivery performance (high).
7. **Maintainer-held integration** (fork and pull, emailed patches). Contributors R and S to their
   own copies; only maintainers perform J — the gate is identity, write access. Raises whether an
   assistant should be a contributor without write access to the line. Fails: maintainer
   bottleneck, bus factor of one (high).
8. **Checkpoint commits, curated before sharing** (Aider auto-commit, Jujutsu, local history). R
   continuous and automatic, undoable and private; the gate moves to curation before S or J.
   Protects against lost work and an assistant's wrong turn. Conflicts with families 1 and 3, where
   every checkpoint would land on the shared line. Fails: curation skipped, rewritten pushed
   history across machines, secrets captured before anyone looks (medium).

**When the one making changes is an assistant.** Hosted agents are kept off the shared line by
design: GitHub's Copilot coding agent works on its own branch, opens a draft PR, the requester
cannot be the approver, and CI does not run on its pushes until a human approves (high). Local
agents (Claude Code, Aider, Cursor) converge on R cheap and automatic, S gated, J through PR —
because S is the first act visible to others, triggers CI or deploys, and is hard to retract
(medium). Agent-specific failures: diffs too large to review, destructive git operations on the
agent's initiative, secrets committed, prompt injection reaching exfiltration once it can push
(medium). Families 1 and 3 let the author choose review; platforms never give an agent Ship.

### What the tasking smuggled in

1. Three movements as the right cut — family 6 needs four, family 8 makes R continuous, family 4's S
   reaches no shared copy, family 7's J is someone else's act.
2. A gate as prior agreement — families 1 and 5 protect after or beside the act: detection, revert,
   the bot's post-merge test.
3. Agreement as the mechanism — others use reversibility or identity.
4. **The two switches as the axes: none of the families puts its main gate on R or S; most put it
   on J. The switches gate the two acts the families leave free, and say nothing of J, which in
   the current arrangement happens silently inside R.**
5. The remote and the line others build on as one place — on a personal branch S touches no shared
   line; across two computers S is synchronisation, not publishing.
6. *A few others soon* invites choosing for a future team; family 1 holds the solo trunk a correct
   rung, not a stage.
7. One assistant for one person — worktrees and requester-cannot-approve bite only with several.
8. Release as a property of the shared line — family 6 denies it.

### Nothing to take

Refused for want of a structural mapping: environment branches (deployment promotion), monorepo
against polyrepo (how many lines, not how movements reach them), pair or ensemble programming
(changes who is present at R; it sits inside family 1). Agent-specific claims rest on practitioner
reports and vendor docs; no source addresses the solo-with-assistant case directly.

## Discovery of situations, 2026-10-14

The user: *the project does not name its scenario; it wants to learn which scenarios exist* — and
asked for a discovery on them. A second pass, separate from the first and not given it, told only
that a person working with an assistant in git wants to learn what ways of working exist before

### Situations

Five things change the practice: who is hurt when the main line breaks, whether more than one
released version must be kept alive, how many writers change the code at once (people and
assistants), whether the writers trust each other, and how many places the work lives in. 1–5 are
bases, 6–9 overlays.

1. **One person, nothing depends on main** (sketch, prototype). Commit straight to main, often —
   least ceremony, history as undo; fails with rare or huge *wip* commits. A branch per
   experiment — cheap to drop; stale branches. Push to a private remote as backup — secrets leak
   into history and become live when it goes public. Committing rarely fails badly once an
   assistant edits many files at once (high).
2. **One person, something runs from main** (deployed site, tool relied on). Commit to main, CI,
   deploy from main, tag per deploy, undo by revert — needs trustworthy tests. Short branch and a
   PR to yourself with CI as the gate — ceremony without a second reviewer. A production branch
   promoted to — drifts, fixes not merged back (medium-high).
3. **Small trusted team, shared main, continuous deploy.** Trunk-based with flags and strong CI
   (DORA correlation, not cause); GitHub Flow — review latency, skimmed large PRs; merge strategy
   (squash, merge, rebase) trade-offs; merge queue and required checks for *each green alone, red
   together*; Ship/Show/Ask, pairing; stacked diffs, most writing from vendors (medium-high).
4. **More than one released version alive** (libraries, apps, on-premises). Release branches with
   fixes on main first and cherry-picked back; Git Flow, which its author says does not suit
   continuous delivery; tags, semver and changelogs automated from commit conventions; release
   trains (high).
5. **Open contribution from the untrusted.** Fork and PR with secrets withheld from forks
   (*pwn requests*); patches by email or Gerrit; CONTRIBUTING, CODEOWNERS, DCO, signed commits;
   project rules on AI-generated contributions — Gentoo, NetBSD, QEMU restrict them (medium-high).
6. **Overlay: several machines** (cloud environments and remote agent sessions count). Push at
   the end of every session, pull at the start, uncommitted state treated as gone — forgotten
   pushes, diverged histories (`pull.rebase` helps); a WIP branch — force-push means resetting the
   other machine; syncing `.git` through Dropbox or OneDrive corrupts it; mixed systems need
   `.gitattributes` (high).
7. **Overlay: experimental work** (research, ML, data). A branch or tag per experiment, dead ends
   kept; results tied to a commit; data and models out of git; notebooks diff badly; the prototype
   quietly becomes the product (medium-high).
8. **Overlay: one assistant makes much of the change.** Start each task from a clean committed
   tree, review the diff, commit or discard — fails when the person's uncommitted edits mix with
   the assistant's and one reset wipes both. The assistant commits small steps itself — noisy;
   messages claiming more than the diff. Its own branch or worktree and a PR — overhead on small
   tasks. Tool checkpoints — not durable, miss shell changes. Destructive git commands run by the
   assistant have destroyed uncommitted work in documented cases; instructions alone are reported
   unreliable, hooks and deny-lists help. Review volume becomes the binding constraint (medium-high).
9. **Overlay: several assistants in parallel.** A worktree and branch per agent, merged by a
   person in dependency order — now the common baseline; conflicts move to merge time and in
   meaning. Split tasks by module. Cloud agents opening PRs behind CI and a merge queue. **Several
   agents in one shared checkout fails by agents overwriting or resetting each other's work
   (high).**

Also changing practice: large binary assets (LFS, locking, Perforce), regulated work, very large
monorepos. Refused as changing nothing: language, host, code size below monorepo scale, private
against public except secrets and licence, commit-message convention unless tooling reads it.

### Moving between situations

Each move breaks an assumption the old situation made silently. Sketch to deployed: broken
commits reach users, no tags to roll back to, old secrets now live. Solo to a second person:
force-pushes destroy others' work, conventions never written, the project does not set up from the
repository alone. One machine to several: diverged histories, work left on the other machine.
Private to public: secrets and large files need a history rewrite. Continuous to supported
releases: no versioning discipline, breaking changes now cost others. One assistant to several:
the shared checkout breaks, and the person's capacity to review. Prototype to product: carry,
squash or start afresh. A team that shrinks keeps the process after its reason is gone.

### How a newcomer is helped to choose

Works: **questions rather than named models — who is hurt if main breaks, must more than one
version be supported, how many writers change the code at once, how fast can a bad change be
undone**; Pro Git's workflow chapter; starting from the host's default and moving only when
something hurts; learning the undo paths before branching strategy; knowing which choices are
cheap to change (most, solo) and which are not (history rewrites, going public, promises to users).
Misleads: Git Flow as the *professional* setup; large-organisation practice applied to one person;
vendor content as neutral comparison; DORA correlations read as cause; *never commit to main* as
universal; command tutorials without reasons; git taken for GitHub; **the assistant's own habits
becoming the workflow by inertia**.

### What the tasking smuggled in

*They use git* fixes the tool — Jujutsu removes the *forgot to commit before the agent ran*
failure. *Ways of working with version control* invites a menu of branching models, when much that
decides outcomes is undo, backup, CI, review capacity, rollback. *Which way suits them* suggests a
fit chosen once, when the fit follows a situation that changes. *Situations* presumes discrete
ones; bases and overlays are partly imposed. Assistants listed beside team size invites treating
them as collaborators — **one person with assistants stays the sole reviewer and integrator, a
different situation from a team of the same headcount**. The listed dimensions anchored the
search; trust, binary assets and regulation were added.
