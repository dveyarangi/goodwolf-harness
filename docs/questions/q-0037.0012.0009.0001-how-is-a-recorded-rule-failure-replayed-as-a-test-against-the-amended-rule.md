## Where it stands

The pass extracts one portable folder under `docs/cases` per historical failure occurrence,
including each repeat in `docs/rule-failures.md`, from original chats, including Claude Code
transcripts. Each folder contains all source material needed to inspect and reproduce its case;
register links reach it without machine-local transcript dependencies. Its provenance always
records the host, the source-reported model of the failing turn, timestamp and chat/message
identifiers, with unavailable attribution explicit rather than inferred.

A case has its own replay start, necessary initial state and replay boundaries. Source evidence
is broader than actor input: the recorded failure and subsequent correction remain evidence,
not hints supplied to the reproducing model. Replay context preserves the necessary original
messages and tool results verbatim, in order and with their roles, without an authored summary.
The case holds the expected behaviour, its source-grounded checks, and clearly attributed
constructed controls whose fitness for those checks is independently reviewed.

Instruction versions belong to an experiment; the case is independent of a particular amendment.
Original evidence remains unchanged. In its experiment copy, preparation may substitute the
tested instruction only at explicitly identified fragments, recording every substitution.
An experiment retains its exact input, criteria, responses and evaluations; changed input or
criteria requires a new run, while earlier results remain historical. The accepted comparison
conditions, repeats and calibrated blind judging remain those of the
[parent experiment](q-0037.0012.0009-how-is-an-amendment-to-an-instruction-tested-before-it-lands-against-which-cases-chosen.md#where-it-stands).

Missing or insufficient source is an evidence gap. Executor inability is recorded separately:
the recovered case stays in the common corpus and is scored neither passed nor failed.
The sequence is one extracted case, independent validation of source fidelity, context
sufficiency and check discrimination, then extraction of the remaining occurrences. Recoverable
evidence is retained and work continues past explicit gaps.

The user accepted this whole shape on 2026-11-09. Execution-tooling development remains outside
the extraction pass. Compatibility with the existing runner is required; the current recovered
material and combined-input runner do not establish implementation of this agreed boundary.

## Boundary assessment, 2026-10-29

### Impact

The candidate under discussion separates evidence of a historical occurrence from the
configuration of a particular instruction comparison. It is not an accepted design.
The existing runner reads both from one case JSON: its validator requires the situation,
message, returned material, instruction variants, target paragraph, judging text and
calibration pairs. Its staging and judging functions consume different subsets. A change
to that input boundary therefore reaches the validator, staging, judging and their fixtures,
not only the extraction script. The existing working case and its recorded experiment supply
the compatibility example. The accepted parent design already requires cases usable for
instruction comparisons and future capture of noticed failures; transcript recovery and live
capture need a common meaning of a case.

### Hidden edges

A historical response documents one occurrence; a new response is an observation under a
chosen instruction and model. The source model and the experiment's requested model are
different facts. Historical instructions can contain material supplied only after the failure;
that material is reference evidence, not replay input. Judging criteria and their calibration
can change when the intended behaviour changes, so their version cannot silently replace an
earlier result's criteria. The current adapter permits text responses, not actual file edits
or command execution. An unsupported replay is distinct from missing source evidence.

### Leave alone

The accepted three-condition comparison, calibrated blind judging and preserved historical
run evidence remain the baseline. No runner extension, new host capability, storage layout,
case schema or conversion of the recovered corpus is authorized by this assessment. Existing
experiment inputs, fingerprints and results remain historical evidence.

### Recommendation

Narrow: settle the boundary and its readers before choosing files or converting more cases.
Use the existing text-response example to explain the consequence of that decision; account
explicitly for recovered occurrences the present adapter cannot replay. Neither reusing the
present combined JSON unchanged nor introducing separate records is decided here.

## Outside accounts of learning from failures, 2026-10-29

The fresh reasoning process read no repository or other task. Its question was:
"A team wants to learn from observed failures by a software assistant and assess proposed
changes to its operating instructions. What substantially different approaches address that
need, and what must be retained or reconstructed under each?" It was asked for alternative
families, their progression, failure modes, confidence and the assumptions transmitted by the
question, without recommending a schema, storage layout, tool or preferred family. The framing
still foregrounded instruction changes and called the observed outcomes failures.

The returned material follows verbatim:

```text
## Families

These are alternative ways to organize the learning, with different standards of success. Confidence concerns the conceptual mapping; none establishes effectiveness in the uninspected project.

**1. Behavioral selection: keep instructions that produce better outcomes.**

- **Mapping and progression:** An observed failure supplies a hypothesis and possible test case. A proposed instruction change becomes a candidate whose behavior is compared across cases. The described need sits between finding a defect and establishing whether a revision improves performance beyond that example. **Confidence: high.**
- **What must survive:** Enough context to reproduce the relevant behavior, the original instructions, the proposed revision, the basis for judging outcomes, and evidence about how cases were selected. Claims of broader improvement require cases beyond the failures that motivated the change. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Teaching to familiar examples; improving a measured behavior while worsening an unmeasured one; treating selected failures as representative; attributing variable outcomes to the instruction change without an adequate comparison. **Confidence: high.**
- **Disagreement retained:** Success here means observed performance improves under the chosen conditions. It need not explain the original failure or justify the underlying standard. **Confidence: high.**

**2. Deductive assurance: make the assistant’s obligations explicit and establish what follows.**

- **Mapping and progression:** The failure is evidence that an obligation, an operational model, or the assistant’s conduct was inadequate. Revision proceeds from specifying the obligation through examining conflicts and establishing which behaviors it permits or excludes. The described need sits before one knows whether the instruction itself was defective or merely violated. **Confidence: high.**
- **What must survive:** The obligation being protected, its scope and exceptions, relevant competing obligations, assumptions about the assistant and environment, and a trace sufficient to establish a violation or counterexample. The exact historical wording matters when interpreting what applied then. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Establishing a property of an abstraction that omits the actual difficulty; making instructions internally consistent but unusable; confusing a stronger statement of duty with a stronger means of ensuring compliance. **Confidence: high.**
- **Disagreement retained:** A sound obligation can remain worth preserving despite imperfect compliance. Conversely, better observed performance does not establish that the instructions express acceptable obligations. **Confidence: high.**

**3. Systemic incident learning: reconstruct how the outcome became possible.**

- **Mapping and progression:** The assistant operates within a system of prompts, tools, permissions, information, interfaces, and human expectations. The failure initiates reconstruction of that system’s behavior; instruction revision is one possible intervention whose relevance remains to be established. The described need sits at the opening of investigation, before selecting the intervention. **Confidence: high.**
- **What must survive:** The consequential sequence, information available at each point, actual constraints and affordances, tool responses, and the expectations against which the outcome was judged. Where these are missing, distinguish reconstruction from contemporaneous evidence. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Writing a persuasive retrospective story; selecting one convenient cause; explaining the assistant through information it did not possess; adding instructions to compensate for defects elsewhere. **Confidence: high.**
- **Disagreement retained:** Preventing recurrence may require changing the surrounding system while leaving operating instructions unchanged. Historical reconstruction alone cannot establish that a proposed intervention will work. **Confidence: high.**

**4. Case-based deliberation: develop defensible judgments through cases and distinctions.**

- **Mapping and progression:** A failure becomes a disputed case: what should the assistant have done, and why? A proposed instruction expresses a judgment intended to govern comparable situations. Progress runs through competing interpretations, reasons, counterexamples, and boundaries of applicability. The described need sits before the failure’s lesson has been settled. **Confidence: high.**
- **What must survive:** Material circumstances, competing interpretations, the reasons supporting the judgment, rejected alternatives, and distinctions that would change the answer. Reconstructing behavior alone is insufficient if the dispute concerns what ought to count as success. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Turning an exceptional case into a universal command; accumulating contradictory precedents; treating an authoritative judgment as evidence of behavioral effectiveness; preserving conclusions while losing their qualifying reasons. **Confidence: high.**
- **Disagreement retained:** Context-sensitive judgment may resist reduction to a uniform instruction. Agreement on acceptable conduct and evidence that an assistant can reliably achieve it are separate achievements. **Confidence: high.**

## What the tasking smuggled in

- **“Failure” sounds settled.** It may instead contain disagreement about intent, acceptable tradeoffs, or whose standard governs. **Confidence: high.**
- **Instructions are foregrounded as the intervention.** Their causal importance, and whether they are the appropriate control surface, remain unestablished. **Confidence: high.**
- **Past failures are assumed to contain transferable lessons.** Some may depend on circumstances that will not recur; others may reveal a broader problem. Which applies requires evidence. **Confidence: high.**
- **Assessment sounds like one question.** Coherence, acceptability, causal effect, and predictive performance are different questions; a procedure answering one may leave the others open. **Confidence: high.**
- **Retention is assumed to solve future interpretation.** Recorded material still requires judgments about relevance and comparability. **Confidence: high.**
- I could not test whether the project already distinguishes these questions, preserves adequate evidence, permits interventions beyond instructions, or has an accepted evaluation procedure.

## Nothing to take

No preferred family, storage arrangement, or evaluation procedure follows from this framing. These distinctions provide material for choosing what kind of claim the team needs to establish; they provide no verdict on any proposed instruction change.
```

## Outside accounts of recording sufficiency, 2026-11-10

A fresh process read no repository or previous pass. Its question was how a team turning past
software-assistant incidents into repeatable tests determines whether enough of the original
situation was retained, and distinguishes missing evidence from missing ability to execute.
The framing retained incidents, recordings and repeatable tests, without proposing a format,
architecture, tool or verdict. It was asked for alternative conceptual families, their
progression, failure modes, confidence and the assumptions the framing transmitted.
Its unmodified material follows; it is not a verdict on these cases.

```text
## Families

These are alternative lenses. Each asks a different question about what a recording must preserve; none establishes that any particular recordings are adequate.

### 1. Historical reconstruction: can the incident be known?

- **Structural mapping:** The incident is a past event; recordings are surviving sources; a reconstructed situation is an account supported by those sources. Provenance, omissions, contradictions, and independent corroboration determine which claims the account can sustain. **Confidence: high in the mapping.**
- **Progression:** Surviving material → warranted event claims → bounded reconstruction. The tasking establishes only that recordings exist; their position beyond that first stage is unknown. **Confidence: high about this evidential limit.**
- **Evidence versus execution:** An unknown instruction is an evidential gap. A known instruction that the present testing environment cannot deliver is an execution gap. These can coexist. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Treating a summary as a complete record; mistaking silence for absence; combining sources from different moments; filling gaps with what would ordinarily have happened. A detailed reconstruction can remain partly conjectural. **Confidence: high as general risks; unknown applicability here.**

This family permits a useful conclusion that other families sometimes obscure: “we cannot establish what happened” can be the right answer even when a plausible test can be run.

### 2. Causal inference: can the failure-producing conditions be recreated?

- **Structural mapping:** The incident is an observed outcome arising from interacting conditions. A regression test is an intervention intended to expose a relevant causal dependency again. The recording supplies observations; it does not automatically identify which conditions caused the outcome. **Confidence: high.**
- **Progression:** Observed association → candidate causal explanation → discriminating interventions → evidence that the explanation transfers to the intended testing conditions. Merely possessing an incident recording places the team before causal identification, unless additional evidence establishes more. **Confidence: high conditionally; actual position unknown.**
- **Evidence versus execution:** Not knowing whether an omitted instruction influenced the incident is an identification problem. Knowing its influence but being unable to recreate the relevant condition is an intervention problem. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Preserving incidental details while omitting a cause; recreating the symptom through a different cause; changing several conditions together; interpreting one successful replay as proof of causal sufficiency; assuming a dependency survives changes in surrounding conditions. **Confidence: high as general risks; unknown applicability here.**

This family makes “enough retained” relative to a causal claim, rather than to resemblance between two transcripts.

### 3. Conformance and counterexamples: can the incident become a valid behavioral test?

- **Structural mapping:** A system exhibits behavior; a governing requirement divides acceptable from unacceptable behavior; the incident may supply a counterexample. A test must retain enough of that counterexample to exercise the requirement and distinguish compliance from violation. **Confidence: high.**
- **Progression:** Recorded behavior → established violated requirement → testable counterexample → evidence that a test discriminates the relevant behaviors. The tasking supplies no governing requirement or established discriminator, so those stages remain unestablished. **Confidence: high.**
- **Evidence versus execution:** Missing evidence about the applicable requirement or the decisive behavior prevents a justified test judgment. An established counterexample that cannot be presented to the system concerns execution. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Turning the historical answer into the required answer; equating any visible difference with failure; testing a convenient surrogate requirement; accepting a test that both faulty and corrected behavior pass; treating one counterexample as coverage of the whole requirement. **Confidence: high as general risks; unknown applicability here.**

This family can accept a test that does not recreate the original incident closely, provided the retained counterexample still establishes the relevant violation.

### 4. Information sufficiency: can relevant distinctions still be recovered?

- **Structural mapping:** A recording is a projection of a richer situation. Different original situations can produce the same retained record. Adequacy depends on whether those indistinguishable originals would require different test conditions or different judgments. **Confidence: high.**
- **Progression:** Specify the distinction the test must preserve → identify what the recording distinguishes → examine unresolved alternatives. Without a stated distinction, “complete enough” has no determinate meaning. The tasking has not supplied one. **Confidence: high.**
- **Evidence versus execution:** If retained material cannot distinguish relevant alternatives, the gap is evidential. If it distinguishes them but the executor cannot express the known distinction, the gap is operational. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Counting recorded fields instead of testing their relevance; assuming a long transcript preserves hidden state; requiring irrelevant fidelity; claiming a compressed record is sufficient without naming what it must preserve; treating a plausible reconstruction as uniquely recoverable. **Confidence: high as general risks; unknown applicability here.**

This family allows apparently sparse evidence to be sufficient for one test purpose and apparently comprehensive evidence to be insufficient for another.

### 5. Experimental reproduction: what exactly must happen again?

- **Structural mapping:** The incident is an experimental result, broadly construed. Different projects may seek recurrence of the exact output, recurrence of the failure category, agreement on the same requirement violation, or stability of an outcome distribution. Those are different reproduction targets. **Confidence: high.**
- **Progression:** Define the target of recurrence → establish the relevant procedure and conditions → conduct repeated trials → assess agreement at the declared level. The tasking reaches toward repetition without establishing which agreement counts. **Confidence: high.**
- **Evidence versus execution:** An undocumented original procedure leaves uncertainty about what would count as repeating it. A documented procedure that cannot presently be performed leaves an execution limitation. An executable repetition with variable outcomes raises a further question about the target and its variability. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Demanding exact output when the intended property permits variation; accepting category-level resemblance when a particular sequence matters; treating one nonrecurrence as disproof; confusing a changed procedure with evidence that the original account was wrong. **Confidence: high as general risks; unknown applicability here.**

This family treats repeatability as a claim requiring a declared level of agreement, rather than a property a recording simply possesses.

### 6. Measurement validity: does the test detect the thing the incident matters for?

- **Structural mapping:** The concern is a construct—such as respecting a boundary or recovering correctly after an error. The incident is one manifestation. A test turns that concern into observable judgments; its validity depends on the relationship between those judgments and the concern. **Confidence: high.**
- **Progression:** Name the concern → identify observable manifestations → establish a defensible judgment → examine misleading positives and negatives. A past incident supplies a candidate manifestation, not an automatically valid measure. **Confidence: high.**
- **Evidence versus execution:** Insufficient evidence that the recorded behavior expresses the concern is a validity gap. Inability to expose or observe an otherwise justified manifestation is an execution gap. A test can execute perfectly while measuring the wrong thing. **Confidence: high.**
- **Characteristic failures:** Detecting wording instead of behavior; treating reviewer agreement as proof of correctness; measuring the easiest visible symptom; allowing incidental presentation changes to alter the judgment; preserving the historical example while losing the concern it exemplified. **Confidence: high as general risks; unknown applicability here.**

This family can find a faithful replay inadequate because historical fidelity alone does not establish that the resulting test measures the intended concern.

## What the tasking smuggled in

- **That the incident is already established as a failure.** A report, an unexpected outcome, and a justified requirement violation are different starting points. **Confidence: high that the framing leaves this unresolved.**
- **That a recording contains a recoverable situation.** It may preserve only one participant’s view, a summary, or selected observations. **Confidence: high as a possibility; no claim about these recordings.**
- **That each incident should become a repeatable test.** Some incidents may support a narrower test, several competing tests, or only a bounded historical finding. **Confidence: high conceptually; particular disposition unknown.**
- **That adequacy is intrinsic to the recording.** Across these families, adequacy depends on the claim or distinction the test must support. **Confidence: high.**
- **That missing evidence and missing execution ability exhaust the obstacles.** Unsettled requirements, causal ambiguity, invalid judgments, and an undefined reproduction target can remain even with extensive evidence and capable execution. **Confidence: high.**
- **That recurrence would establish recovery of the original situation.** The same visible outcome can arise through different conditions; conversely, relevant conditions can recur without producing an identical output. **Confidence: high generally; mechanisms here unknown.**
- **That present executability is permanent.** An execution limitation is relative to available capabilities and accessible conditions. Some historical conditions may also be irretrievable. **Confidence: high generally; recoverability here unknown.**
- **That the recordings describe any particular product’s actual behavior or capabilities.** No product or version claim has been checked. **Confidence: high about the absence of established evidence in this task.**

## Nothing to take

No conclusion follows about the adequacy of the available recordings, the feasibility of replay, the validity of a proposed test, or the cause of any incident. These families supply competing questions to ask of evidence; they supply no verdict on an uninspected project.
```
