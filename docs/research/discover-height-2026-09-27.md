### Families

**1. Requirements engineering — requirement levels and use-case goal levels** (Lauesen, Wiegers, Cockburn). *Ladder:* Lauesen's goal-level → domain-level (user's tasks) → product-level (what the system does at its interface) → design-level (how it's built); Wiegers' business → user → functional/non-functional; Cockburn's summary ("cloud/kite") → user-goal ("sea level") → subfunction ("fish/clam"). *Mapping:* intent≈goal/business; behaviour≈product/functional; action≈design. Lauesen's **domain level** (what the human does with the system) has no counterpart — it sits where "strategy" is contemplated, but holds tasks, not approach. (high) *Known failures:* "one person's what is another person's how" (A. Davis) — the requirement/design boundary is viewer-relative (high, principle); Swartout & Balzer 1982, specification and implementation are inevitably intertwined, so a spec can't be finished before design choices (high, argued from cases); Cockburn names writing at too low a level — UI clicks and field names in a user-goal case — the commonest use-case fault (high, practitioner evidence); Cockburn keeps a use case's *goal level* separate from its *level of detail* ("casual" vs "fully dressed") — two axes, not one (high).

**2. Means–ends abstraction hierarchy** (Rasmussen; Vicente, *Cognitive Work Analysis*). *Ladder:* functional purpose → abstract function (values, priorities, balances) → generalized function → physical function → physical form; any level is the *why* of the one below and the *how* of the one above. *Mapping:* intent=functional purpose; strategy≈abstract function; behaviour≈generalized function; action≈physical function/form. Rasmussen crosses this with an orthogonal part–whole decomposition axis. (high) *Known failures:* practitioners conflate means–ends ("why/how") with part–whole ("bigger/smaller piece"), so a smaller part is taken for a lower level (medium; stated in the CWA literature, not measured); levels are relational — a statement's level is fixed by its neighbours, not its content (high, principle).

**3. Military orders and mission command** (US ADP/FM 6-0; Auftragstaktik). *Ladder:* commander's intent = purpose + key tasks + **end state**; concept of operations / scheme of maneuver (the chosen approach); tasks to subordinate units (actions); above it, levels of war (strategic/operational/tactical). *Mapping:* intent=purpose; *strategy=concept of operations* — a well-populated rung here; behaviour=end state; action=tasks. Doctrine puts end state *inside* intent, not a level below it. (high) *Known failures:* intent written as restated mission or task list (medium, doctrinal commentary); Shattuck (Military Review, 2000) found in exercises that subordinates frequently could not reconstruct the commander's intent (medium, evidence, small studies); over-specified orders ("detailed command") suppress initiative when the plan breaks (high as doctrine, principle); "say what and why, not how; understand two levels up" treats levels as relative to echelon (high).

**4. Results chains — logframe, theory of change, OKRs.** *Ladder:* logframe Goal → Purpose → Outputs → Activities (→ Inputs), each row with indicators, means of verification, and **assumptions**; theory of change impact → outcomes → outputs → activities; OKR objective → key results → initiatives. *Mapping:* intent=goal/purpose/objective; behaviour=outcome *or* output; action=activities/initiatives. (high) *Known failures:* key results written as tasks — outputs mistaken for outcomes, the best-known OKR fault (medium-high, practitioner evidence from Doerr and Wodtke, not controlled studies); outputs vs outcomes — "observably true when done" can mean *we shipped X* or *users now achieve Y*, two different rungs (high); "lockframe" — the ladder ossifies into a contract (medium); Goodhart's law acts on whichever rung carries the indicators (high, principle); logframe's "vertical logic" treats the link between levels, with its assumptions, as the thing to check, not the level labels (high).

**5. Refinement and problem frames** (Wirth/Dijkstra stepwise refinement; VDM/Z/B; Jackson, Zave & Jackson 1997). *Ladder:* refinement is a relation, not a fixed ladder — any number of steps, each proven against the one above; Jackson separates requirement R (in the world), specification S (at the machine interface), program P, with domain knowledge W such that W, S ⊢ R. *Mapping:* intent≈R; behaviour≈S; action≈P. (high) *Known failures:* "implementation bias" in specifications — the spec commits to one design (Jones, VDM) (high); Zave & Jackson — moving from R to S is a *change of subject* (world vs interface) needing domain assumptions W, not added detail, and omitting W is the classic error (high, argued); the number of levels is arbitrary (high).

**6. Behaviour-driven development and impact mapping** (Adzic; Cucumber community). *Ladder:* BDD feature narrative ("in order to…") → scenarios (Given/When/Then) → step definitions/code; impact mapping why (goal) → who (actors) → how (**change in actors' behaviour**) → what (deliverables). *Mapping:* intent=narrative/goal; behaviour=scenarios; action=steps/code — but in impact mapping "behaviour" means *people's* behaviour and sits *above* deliverables. (high) *Known failures:* "imperative" scenarios (click, type, press) instead of "declarative" — the exact wrong-level fault, widely documented in Cucumber guidance (high, practitioner evidence); incidental detail in examples (high); "behaviour" is ambiguous between system behaviour and actor behaviour (high).

**7. Enterprise architecture perspectives and model-driven levels** (Zachman; OMG MDA). *Ladder:* Zachman rows scope/planner → business/owner → system/designer → technology/builder → component/subcontractor, crossed with what/how/where/who/when/why columns; MDA CIM (computation-independent) → PIM (platform-independent) → PSM (platform-specific). *Mapping:* intent≈scope/business or CIM; behaviour≈system model or PIM; action≈technology/component or PSM. (high) *Known failures:* Zachman says outright that rows are *perspectives for different audiences, not levels of detail* — each row can be detailed or coarse (high, his repeated claim); in MDA "platform" is relative, so the PIM/PSM line moves (medium); model transformations rarely held in practice (medium, evidence from adoption history).

**8. Action description and construal level** (Anscombe, *Intention*; Davidson's "accordion effect"; Goldman's level-generation; Vallacher & Wegner's action identification theory; Trope & Liberman's construal level theory). *Ladder:* one act has many descriptions linked by "in order to" — moving an arm → pumping → replenishing the water supply → poisoning; levels are descriptions of the *same* act, on a continuum. *Mapping:* intent/behaviour/action = three cuts through one "why?/how?" chain. (high) *Known failures:* Vallacher & Wegner, experimentally — people shift to lower-level identifications when an action is difficult or failing and to higher ones when it's fluent, so authors under difficulty drift to action level (medium, experimental evidence, lab settings); construal level — distance, temporal or social, produces abstract descriptions and proximity concrete ones, so documents written far from execution skew abstract (medium, evidence, with a live replication debate); there are no natural bins, and any three-way cut is conventional (high, principle).

**9. AI planning and belief–desire–intention agents** (STRIPS/HTN; Bratman). *Ladder:* classical planning goal *state* (a declarative condition) vs plan (a procedural operator sequence); HTN compound task → methods (a choice of approach) → primitive operators; Bratman's intentions as *partial plans* filled in over time. *Mapping:* behaviour=goal state; strategy=method choice; action=operator sequence. Intent-as-why has no slot in classical planning; in BDI it is desire/intention. (high) *Known failures:* behaviour and action differ *in kind* (state vs process), not in detail, and conflating them is a known modelling error (high, principle); partial plans are about *commitment* — what is fixed vs still open — as much as about level (medium).

**10. Curriculum design — backward design and behavioural objectives** (Tyler; Mager; Wiggins & McTighe). *Ladder:* desired results → acceptable evidence → learning plan; Mager's objectives as performance, condition, criterion. *Mapping:* intent=desired results; behaviour=acceptable evidence (observable performance); action=learning plan — a near one-to-one fit. (high) *Known failures:* the "twin sins" of activity-driven and coverage-driven design, i.e. starting at the action rung (high, practitioner literature); the behavioural-objectives movement was criticised (Eisner and others) for forcing every aim into observable behaviour, losing aims that are real but not directly observable, so intent shrinks to what can be tested (high that the critique exists; its merit is argued).

**11. Law and hierarchy of norms** (purpose clause → statute → regulation → guidance/procedure; rules vs standards). *Ladder:* purpose → rules → procedure. *Mapping:* intent=legislative purpose; behaviour=substantive rule (what must hold); action=implementing regulation/procedure. (medium) *Known failures:* purposive vs textual interpretation — which level governs when they conflict (high); Kaplow's rules (detail fixed in advance) vs standards (detail filled in when applied), an explicit trade-off of *where* detail is placed and who fixes it (high); gold-plating when rules are transposed downward (medium).

**12. V-model verification pairing.** *Ladder:* requirements↔acceptance test, system spec↔system test, architecture↔integration test, detailed design↔unit test. *Mapping:* intent↔acceptance/validation; behaviour↔system test; action↔design/unit test — each level is defined by *how it is checked*. (high) *Known failures:* levels conflated with time-ordered phases, yielding waterfall (high); several design rungs exist where the candidate has one "action" (high).

### What the tasking smuggled in

1. **"Levels of detail" as the axis's nature.** Families 2, 5, 7 and 9 each say the axis is something else — means–ends (why/how), change of subject (world vs interface), audience/perspective (Zachman: explicitly not detail), or kind (state vs process).
2. **One linear axis.** Rasmussen (means–ends × part–whole), Cockburn (goal level × formality) and Zachman (rows × interrogatives) all have two; "detail" of a document and "level" of a statement may be separate axes.
3. **Absolute, fixed levels.** Families 1, 2, 3, 5 and 8 treat level as relative to the adjacent statement — "one person's what is another's how" — so the same intent/behaviour/action triple recurs at every scale. The use "catch a statement at the wrong level" presumes level is readable from a statement in isolation.
4. **A small fixed count (three, maybe four).** Refinement and action description give open-ended levels; military, HTN and Lauesen populate the intent–behaviour rung the tasking judged empty.
5. **"A rung exists only if a current document sits only there."** Evidence from one method's documents shaping an axis meant to be method-neutral.
6. **"Action" = concrete steps, while the worked example below behaviour is "types and fields"** — structure/design, not steps. The candidate's lowest rung merges design (structure) with plan (procedure), which families 7, 9 and 12 separate.
7. **"Behaviour" as one rung** hides system output vs user outcome (4) and system vs actor behaviour (6). The name also imports BDD's sense and British spelling.
8. **"Intent" as a rung separate from its end state** — doctrine (3) puts end state *inside* intent, blurring the intent/behaviour boundary.
9. **Documents "sit" at a level, while the uses speak of statements.** In every family real documents mix levels (an operations order carries intent *and* tasks). The tasking holds both framings without choosing.
10. **Uses that are all classification and policing.** No use concerns the *link* between levels (logframe vertical logic, V-model pairing, W,S ⊢ R), who authors each level (audience/authority), or what is committed vs left open (Bratman, rules vs standards).
11. **That method-neutrality is achievable.** MDA's and the logframe's ladders are meaningful only together with their method's transformations or checks.
12. **Top-down authorship by "people who direct"** — a commander/subordinate split, so the axis is quietly also an axis of authority.
13. **Asking where the candidate "sits on each family's ladder"** invites confirmatory mapping onto the candidate rather than asking whether its rungs survive.

### Nothing to take

Refused families: runtime layering (OSI, code abstraction layers) — layers of a running system, not descriptions of one piece of work; C4 model (context/container/component/code) — part–whole zoom only, no why/how dimension; Bloom's taxonomy — ranks cognitive complexity of learning, not descriptions of one task; DIKW pyramid — its rungs are different kinds of things, not one work item described at different levels; cartographic scale/generalization — same territory at different resolution, levels differing only in resolution, not in kind; Sinek's "golden circle" (why/how/what) — mappable, but no body of knowledge on failure modes to contribute. Low-weight: construal-level effects on document authoring are extrapolated from lab studies; the MDA PIM/PSM critique is from recalled adoption history, not a cited source.
