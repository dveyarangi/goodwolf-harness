PART: Who decides which side is right when landed code differs from an edge record's Contract or Invariants -- E4 has /verify rewrite the record "to what landed", without examining that this is the same direction E2 and the /edge Validate goal forbid, or that it sidesteps E3's pre-landing breaking-or-compatible decision.
CONTRACT CHANGE: E4 would stop being "update the record to match the code at /verify". Verify would instead compare the landed work to the record; a mismatch with a promise would go to /align as a contract decision made by the person, and only mechanical updates would be written at verify (remove the roadmap entry, mark stale results). This changes the rule's text, its authority, and the E2/E3/Validate relationships it is installed beside.
REASONING: The draft commits to one new installed rule, E4 in /verify, that makes the landing commit rewrite Contract and Invariants "to what landed", plus re-observation or stale-marking of sidecars. I checked the tree. The claim that no /implement or /verify rule covers edges holds: verify only runs edges.py --check and has a generic "compare to governing docs" clause, so the gap is real. But E2 in edge.rules.md says a promise the code no longer keeps is drift, "never rewritten to fit". The edge skill's Validate goal says which side is wrong is the user's call. Verify's own text says not to rewrite a governing rule to make a violation disappear, and sends contradictions and new decisions to /align. E3 requires a contract change to be named breaking or compatible out loud before proceeding. E4 reverses all of that for the one place where the record binds consumers and extenders: it makes the implementation the source of truth. The draft puts only placement to the person (verify or implement, and the wording). That leaves its premise unexamined, and the premise crosses owners: the record, the align decision gate and the maintain rule. Other things it leaves open are detail, such as how a verifier detects that a change touches an edge (E2's backtick-named files could serve) and whether live re-observation is feasible (the "unobserved since" fallback exists). Those can be settled later without changing the commitment, so they fail the load-bearing test.
