PART: The draft turns one atomic, whole-validated declared line into independent per-event calls and says "nothing the store promised is lost", without examining what the line's single-transaction form guaranteed: all-or-nothing application of a turn's compound events (opens then at, moves then prune, closes, depends), and the parser's enforcement that every turn carries exactly one `at` or `nothing`.
CONTRACT CHANGE: The writer's promise changes from "the whole line is validated against the store before anything is written, and a refusal writes nothing" to per-call validation. A turn that opens, moves, closes and places can now be left half-applied when a middle call is refused. Decision 56's "every turn calls `at`" loses its only enforcement point (no line, no parser check), and the judge's typed clause list (decision 39, one writer with two front doors) would need either a batch path or a separate one. So the slice must either keep a batch or transaction form or restate its outcome and acceptance criteria.
REASONING: The draft commits to replacing `--declare "<line>"` with one subcommand per event. In questions.py, `declare()` applies all clauses to an in-memory draft, checks `at` against the result, rechecks every touched file, then writes. The RFC states "the whole line is validated ... a refusal writes nothing", and `parse()` refuses a line without exactly one `at`. The prune rule is also written around same-line `moves`. The draft's acceptance criteria only say that a bad call writes nothing, which is per call, and its outcome claims nothing is lost. I checked what the impact lists as risks: shell quoting, the id race, and the hook flags that stay. Those are local or already examined. The cross-event atomicity and the per-turn `at` guarantee are not mentioned anywhere in the draft or the earlier exchanges. They sit in the contract between the agent, the writer, the judge's front door (`.0040`) and the later slices `.0012` and `.0015`, so settling them after the slice is minted would change what it promises. The architecture contract does not promise multi-file atomicity, so the loss may be acceptable, but that is a decision the draft skips.
